Case study

Opportunity Radar

An n8n flow which finds companies likely to be hiring before roles are even posted: a live dashboard ranking hiring momentum by tracking signal strength across news, Reddit, LinkedIn, GitHub, Hacker News, funding, and exec hires in real time.

01

The short version

Problem
By the time a job is posted, 100+ people have applied. Hiring leaves signals earlier, but I had no way to see them.
Opportunity
An n8n pipeline that scores companies on eight signal sources, with a dashboard that ranks hiring momentum and surfaces the people worth contacting.
Result
Across 280+ tracked companies and 290+ scans, it surfaced real companies with rising hiring momentum, and the people worth reaching out to.
02

Origin

It started in a coffee shop, mid job search: hiring leaves a trail before the posting.

I built an n8n pipeline that scored companies on hiring signals and filled a spreadsheet.

It worked, but a spreadsheet isn’t a product, and I knew it needed a front end.

The friction that started it: over 100 applicants before most people even see the posting.

Built in the coffee shop: eight signal sources merged, deduped, and scored per company.

03

Decisions

Decision 01

Spreadsheet → a front end

The breakthrough: n8n could export the whole workflow as JSON, and Claude could parse it into a data model, a feature list, and a first working front end.

Tried
Reading the pipeline's output straight from the spreadsheet it filled.
Rejected
It showed the companies and the people, but only as a report that nobody else could pick up and use.
Kept
A front end: a dashboard that ranks companies and shows who to contact.
Why
A UI turns a report into software someone can use.
Then
Claude’s first screens came out in Figma. See Decision 02
Decision 02

Figma → straight into the DOM

I started in Figma, but the browser quickly proved the faster canvas.

Tried
Claude’s first Figma screens, generated from that JSON: a radar chart and a company detail page.
Rejected
Every change meant redrawing in Figma, then rebuilding in code, against data Figma couldn't show.
Kept
Building directly in the browser against the live data, with Claude as co-pilot.
Why
Real data exposed problems no mockup would, and each change took minutes instead of a round trip.
Then
The first build put a colored bar under every row, and the list strained under it. See Decision 03
Decision 03

Bars in every row → a drawer

I tried several ways to fit each company’s signal breakdown into the list, and landed on a drawer you open only when you want it.

Tried
A segmented bar under every row, one color per signal source, then numbers above each segment, then a single combined bar, then a drawer that opened on click.
Rejected
The bars crowded the list and competed with the ranking; the first drawer opened on any row click, mixing up selecting a company with expanding it.
Kept
Clean rows, each with its own toggle to open the breakdown, and the selected company’s score math beside the list.
Why
The list is for comparing companies; the breakdown is for understanding one. They belong to different moments.
Then
In August, I gave the drawer its own toggle, so a click on a row just selects it, and the rail took the score math. See Decision 10
Decision 04

Bubble radar → a leaderboard

The overview chart kept getting asked to do more, so I replaced the radar with a quadrant, then led with a leaderboard that says who’s rising now.

Tried
A bubble radar that placed every company by its score, enlarged and relabeled as it strained, then a Job Value Quadrant on its own.
Rejected
The radar was asked to do too much and buckled: labels collided at real company counts, it mostly repeated the ranked list, and it never said what to do next.
Kept
A Leaderboard of the top 12, colored by momentum, as the default view, with the Job Value Quadrant one tab over.
Why
Each view answers an action: the leaderboard says who’s rising now; the quadrant says who to reach out to before they post a role.
Decision 05

Stealth Hiring, added then cut

I tried a Stealth Hiring badge to surface hidden opportunities, but it added clutter and confusion, so I cut it.

Tried
A Stealth Hiring filter and badge for companies that were funded and hiring executives but had posted few roles.
Rejected
It added clutter, and it confused even me, the person who built the tool.
Chose
Removing it completely instead of hiding it.
Why
A thin public footprint can also mean fewer people to reach, which works against the tool's goal.
Decision 06

A finder → a decision tool

Finding companies wasn’t enough; the next questions were “is this right for me?” and “how do I prepare?”, so Fit and Prep grew out of the work.

Tried
OppRad as a finder: it surfaced companies and who to contact, and stopped there.
Rejected
Finding a company didn't answer whether it was worth pursuing.
Chose
Adding Fit and Prep: Fit first checked for live openings, then judged the company itself, since most had none posted yet; Prep researched the company and interviewer for the interview.
Why
After “who,” the next question is “is this right for me?” Answering it turned a finder into a decision tool.
Then
It started in August as “Triage,” a separate app, moved in four days later, and outgrew the dashboard. See Decision 10
Decision 07

Four columns → two

Fit needed a new layout; this was the one point where I greyboxed layouts, and I went from four columns to two.

Tried
A four-column pipeline: ranked list, shortlist, verified openings, and company details, side by side.
Rejected
It was built around verifying real job postings, which Fit stopped doing.
Chose
Two columns: the ranked list, with the shortlist shrunk to a bar above it, beside the selected company's details.
Why
I greyboxed five layouts first, and with verification gone, one list and one company was all the page needed.
Then
The overview charts came back as a third column. See Decision 09
Decision 08

Shortlist modal → Fit runs in place

I tried Fit in a separate modal, then brought it back inline, and Prep joined it as a peer.

Tried
A modal for building a shortlist and batch-checking up to three companies at once.
Rejected
It put a separate step between seeing a company and checking whether it fit.
Chose
Fit running right inside the selected company, with Prep beside it as a peer.
Why
Checking a company's fit belongs where you're already looking at that company.
Then
Fit and Prep moved out to their own app. See Decision 10
Decision 09

Two columns → three

Two columns lost the big picture, so I restored three, and Close joined Fit and Prep in the rail.

Tried
Two columns: the ranked list beside the selected company's details.
Rejected
It had dropped the leaderboard, the Job Value Quadrant, and the hiring-signal gauge.
Chose
The original three-column layout, with the overview back on the left.
Why
The left column gives the whole field at a glance, before you dig into any one company.
Decision 10

Interview prep becomes its own app

Fit, Prep & Close was turning it into a Swiss Army knife, so I pruned it back to the original idea: finding companies and people worth contacting.

Tried
A Fit, Prep & Close panel in the right rail, to check fit, prep for an interview, and weigh an offer next to each company.
Rejected
It pushed each score's breakdown below the fold.
Kept
Prep in its own app, and a right rail that shows how each score is calculated.
Why
Finding promising companies and preparing for one interview are different jobs.

Outcomes

Contacts surfaced to date

Live and in use, surfacing real companies with rising hiring momentum and who to contact.

The instinct underneath: find the signal, build the smallest system for it, acknowledge its limits.

It shows real contacts, so I share it by walkthrough; get in touch.

Shipped: a ranked leaderboard, plus a company inspector with momentum trends and contacts. Each score shows how it was calculated.

Trade-offs
No Crunchbase API access, so funding-round and headcount data comes from weaker, indirect signals (news mentions, exec-hire activity) instead of the real thing — the first upgrade I'd make with real budget.
No verified email finder either — contacts surface as LinkedIn profiles, not confirmed email addresses, leaving a manual step from "here's the person" to actually reaching them.
